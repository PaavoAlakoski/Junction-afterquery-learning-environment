Delayed Supply Chain

Findings report for the AfterQuery "Break the Agent" challenge, Junction 2026
Team "Rome wasn't built in 48h" · 14 November 2026

Frontier agents score well below a reproducible reference run in this environment, and they lose for a reason that comes from the task: they cut stock to save holding cost and cannot recover when demand rises.

  • On the five default scenarios the reference scores 67.0 out of 100. Claude Haiku 5.5 scored 42.3, 48.3 and 52.7 in three runs; Claude Sonnet 5.5 scored 21.7 in one run.
  • The reference had the lower cost in 18 of the 20 agent scenario runs.
  • Doing nothing, stopping early, flooding the chain, hoarding at the factory and random orders all score 0.
  • A nine-number policy trained on the environment from episode cost alone rises from 0.0 to 62.3, so the reward can be learned from.
  • Open items: the Sonnet result is a single run, and the MCP server has not yet been run with AfterQuery's harness.

1. The setting and what an agent can learn

The agent runs a four-stage supply chain for 52 weeks: factory, distributor, wholesaler, retailer, customers. Each week it chooses four order quantities, one per stage. It is a centralised version of the "beer game" used to teach supply chain management.

We chose it because the difficulty is in the decisions, not in the interface. The rules fit on one page and the action is four whole numbers, yet a production decision reaches a customer nine weeks later, so every choice has to be made against demand the agent cannot yet see. An agent can learn three things here:

  • to act on the whole pipeline of stock and shipments, not on this week's shortage;
  • to tell a lasting change in demand from a temporary one;
  • to decide where in the chain to keep buffer stock, and how much, given that being short costs ten times as much as holding a unit at the retailer.

2. The environment

ElementDefinition
GoalLowest total cost over 52 weeks.
ActionFour order quantities per week, whole numbers from 0 to 200. The factory's order is production, capped at 80 per week. Orders cannot be cancelled.
DelaysShipments take 2, 2, 2 and 3 weeks to reach the retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory. A stage that cannot fill an order ships what it has and owes the rest.
CostsHolding: 1.00, 0.75, 0.50 and 0.25 per unit per week from retailer to factory. Unfilled customer demand: 10 per unit per week.
DemandNoisy around a level that starts between 15 and 25. Every scenario has one lasting change (step up, step down or ramp); half also have a seasonal wave or a short promotion.
Handover ruleThe chain must end with stock covering nine weeks of recent demand; each unit short costs 10. This removes the incentive to run stock down at the end.
ObservationEverything except future demand: stock at every stage, every shipment in transit and its arrival week, all backorders, full demand and order history, and all cost parameters.

Fairness. The rules text given to the agent states the demand model in full, including the ranges for the starting level, the noise, the size and timing window of the lasting change, and the seasonal and promotion effects. The reference uses only that text and the same observation the agent receives.

Reward

For each scenario two anchors are computed on the same demand: naive, where every stage orders what customers bought this week, and floor, a planner that knows future demand exactly.

score = 100 × (naive − agent) / (naive − floor), clipped to 0–100

The suite score pools costs over all scenarios before applying the formula. Matching the naive rule scores 0; scoring 100 would require knowing the future.

3. The reference run

The reference is a base-stock policy driven by a Bayesian demand forecast. It is deterministic and runs with one command.

  • Forecast. It draws 4,000 candidate scenarios from the demand model stated in the rules, weighs each by how well it explains the demand seen so far, and reads off the distribution of demand over the coming weeks.
  • Ordering. Each stage orders up to a high quantile (97–98%) of forecast demand over its cumulative lead time, counting everything already at or on its way to that stage and the stages below it.

Its four service levels were tuned on seeds 1000–1149 and checked on seeds 1150–1249, where it scores 76.4 and 77.4. None of those are evaluation seeds. On ten further seeds (11–20) it scores 77.3, and on 40 seeds never used for any tuning (3000–3039) it scores 78.1.

5. Where the agents fall short, and why

Both models run out of stock. Replaying each agent's orders and splitting the cost shows that the agents spend about the same as the reference on holding stock, or less, and far more on unfilled customer demand.

The mistake connects to three decisions in the task:

  • Trading holding cost against shortage risk. The agents reason, correctly, that stock costs money, and trim it. They underweight that a unit short costs ten times a unit held at the retailer, and 40 times a unit held at the factory.
  • Hedging against a change the rules announce. The rules say a step up of ×1.4–1.9 can arrive between weeks 9 and 35. Sonnet's notes mention watching for it, but it held no stock against it.
  • Acting nine weeks ahead. Once demand has risen, new production takes up to nine weeks to reach customers. Reacting at that point is too late; the stock has to be in the pipeline already.

Example: Sonnet 5.5 on scenario 3. When demand dipped in weeks 13–20, Sonnet ordered almost nothing upstream and let system stock fall from 311 to 187 units; the reference kept it near 300. Demand rose from week 25, the retailer stood empty for 14 weeks, and backlog reached 39 units. A second rise in weeks 48–52 pushed backlog to 115 units with the factory already at its cap of 80. Sonnet's cost was 10,574 against the reference's 5,272.

The stronger model did worse. Sonnet trimmed stock more aggressively than Haiku, which lowered its holding cost and raised its backlog cost by more. This rests on one Sonnet run, so the size of the difference is uncertain.

Other observations. All 260 Sonnet replies and 779 of 780 Haiku replies were valid. One Haiku reply (run 2, scenario 5, week 21) contained notes but no orders and was scored as zero orders, as the rules state. No run paid a handover penalty.

6. Reward integrity

test_exploits.py contains 17 automated checks, all passing:

  • doing nothing, stopping after week 1, 10, 26 or 40, flooding the chain with maximum orders, and hoarding at the factory, and random orders all score 0;
  • stopping in the last few weeks, or dumping stock and refilling in the final week, never beats playing to the end;
  • malformed orders (NaN, negatives, strings, wrong length, booleans) count as zero and are capped at the limits;
  • units are conserved, and results are identical on repeated runs with the same seed;
  • the MCP server gives the same cost as playing the environment directly; an agent that never acts, stops, or sends malformed arguments is scored as placing zero orders; and there is no tool to restart the episode or choose the scenario, so an agent cannot replay a scenario to learn its demand.

If an agent crashes or stops, the remaining weeks are played with zero orders, so stopping early is scored as doing nothing from that point.

Training the small policy in section 7 was also a search for loopholes: 45,000 episodes of optimising cost directly ended below the reference, with ordinary ordering behaviour.

7. Learning from the environment (stretch goal)

train.py trains a small ordering policy from episode cost alone. The policy has nine numbers: two smoothing speeds for the demand history, how far to follow a rise or a fall, a safety margin, and how many weeks of demand each stage keeps. It knows nothing about the demand model in the rules and does not use the reference's forecast. Training is a cross-entropy search over 45,000 episodes on seeds 2000–2059 and takes about two minutes.

The reward gives a usable training signal, what is learned carries over to scenarios never trained on, and the trained policy ends above both Claude models and below the reference. The task is therefore learnable, and the agents' gap is not explained by the task being impossible. This is a small scripted policy; we did not fine-tune a language model, so it answers the stretch goal only in part.

8. Reproducing the results

python evaluate.py                 # reference, baselines and the small policy
python -m unittest -v              # reward-exploit tests
python evaluate.py --agent claude  # three agent runs (needs ANTHROPIC_API_KEY)
python mcp_server.py --seed 1      # serve one scenario to an agent harness over MCP
python train.py                    # retrain the small policy
python replay.py                   # build replay.html, a week-by-week comparison of saved runs

The simulator, baselines and reference need Python 3.10 or later and only the standard library. The reference and baselines are deterministic. Agent runs vary between repetitions; each is saved with its full transcripts to a timestamped file in results/.

9. Limitations

  • Sample size. Five scenarios, three Haiku runs and one Sonnet run. The direction of the gap is consistent; its exact size is not well measured.
  • The reference is not best on every scenario. On scenario 1 it scores 0.0, slightly worse than naive, because it carries stock against a rise in demand that does not come. On the tuning seeds it fails to beat naive on roughly one scenario in seven. Its advantage shows in the pooled score.
  • Agent setup. The agents ran at medium effort without code execution, through the Anthropic API: each week they saw the rules, the state and their own notes. Through the MCP server an agent keeps its whole conversation instead. No agent has been run that way yet, so the gap under the judging harness is unmeasured.
  • Harness. mcp_server.py exposes the environment as an MCP server with four tools (rules, state, place orders, stop), over stdio or HTTP. A full episode has been played through it with the official MCP Python client; it has not yet been run with AfterQuery's harness.
  • The floor is not a proven lower bound. It is a feasible plan made with perfect foresight.
  • Stretch goal. The model we trained is a nine-number policy, not a smaller language model.
